Their goal is to hypothesise relationships between text data and a target variable in a text classifier. They can't directly interpret text encoders because they are too complex. They can't directly interpret the output representations (e.g. via looking at the logistic regression weights) because they are too entangled.

Instead, they develop a method they call HypotheSAE. It has 3 steps:
1) Train an [SAE](./SAE-sparse-autoencoders.md) on text embeddings
2) Select features that predict a target variable
3) Generate explanations for those features

They evaluate HypotheSAE across 3 synthetic tasks and 3 real-world tasks and find that it produces many more significant hypotheses than 3 baselines.

HypotheSAE is also much faster and cheaper than LLM-based methods since the encoder is much smaller.

# Theoretical Framework

Let
- $Z \in \{0, 1\}$ be an indicator for whether a neuron fires for a given text input
- $\hat Z \in \{ 0, 1 \}$ be an indicator for whether the text contains specified concept
- $Y \in \{ 0, 1 \}$ be a target variable

Define the predictiveness of $Z$ and $\hat Z$ as separation scores (as opposed to [explained variance](./variance-explained.md), for example):
$$
\begin{align}
S(Z) & := | \E[Y \mid Z = 1] - \E[Y \mid Z = 0] | \\
S(\hat Z) & := | \E[Y \mid \hat Z = 1] - \E[Y \mid \hat Z = 0] | \\
\end{align}
$$

Define fidelity of $\hat Z$ by
$$
\delta(\hat Z, Z) := \frac{1 - \min(\text{recall}, \text{precision})}{\min(P(\hat Z=0), P(Z=0))}
$$
where
- $\text{precision}$ is how often the neuron fires when the concept is present
- $\text{recall}$ is how often the concept is present when the neuron fires
$$
\text{precision} = \frac{P(\hat Z = 1, Z = 1)}{P(\hat Z = 1)}
\qquad \qquad 
\text{recall} = \frac{P(\hat Z=1, Z=1)}{P(Z=1)}
$$ 
Then, in Proposition 3.1, [they show that](./proof-proposition-3.1-HypotheSAE.md) if $Y$ is supported on $[0,1]$ then
$$
| S(\hat Z) - S(Z) | \leq \delta(\hat Z, Z)
$$
In other words, if $\hat Z$ is a high-fidelity predictor of $Z$ then it will also have a similar separation score.

Proposition 3.1 implies that to find a large $S(\hat Z)$ we can find a good predictive neuron with high $S(Z)$ and a high-fidelity interpretation with high $\delta(\hat Z, Z)$
$$
S(\hat Z)
\geq \underbrace{S(Z)}_{\substack{\text{neuron}\\\text{predictiveness}}}
- \underbrace{\delta(\hat Z, Z)}_{\substack{\text{interpretation}\\\text{fidelity}}}
$$

# Method

They use a variant of [TopK SAEs](./topk-sparse-autoencoders.md):
$$
\begin{align}
z_i & = \text{ReLU}(\text{TopK}(W_\text{enc} (e_i - b_\text{pre}) + b_\text{enc})) \\
\hat e_i & = W_\text{dec} z_i + b_\text{dec}
\end{align}
$$
where
- $e_i \in \R^D$ is the $D$-dimensional text embedding for text $x_i$
- $W_\text{enc} \in \R^{M \times D}$ and $W_\text{dec} \in \R^{D \times M}$ are the encoding and decoding matrices to and from an $M$-dimensional feature space
- $\text{TopK}$ sets all elements except for the first $k$ to 0

They use OpenAI's `text-embedding-3-small` with $D = 1536$ for their embeddings.

They choose $M$ and $k$ based on performance on a validation set. Interestingly, $M$ end up being smaller than $D$ for all 5 datasets!

Training optimisations based on best practices from [Towards Monosemanticity](./towards-monosemanticity.md):
1) Tie $b_\text{pre} = b_\text{dec}$
2) Initialise $b_\text{pre}$ to the geometric median of the training set
3) Initialise $W_\text{dec} = W_\text{enc}^\top$
4) Force all decoder rows to have unit norm

They use the following loss function
$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal L_\text{TopK} & = \| e_i - \hat e_i \|_2^2 \\
\mathcal L_\text{AuxK} & = \| W_\text{dec} z_i^\text{AuxK} - (e_i - \hat e_i) \|_2^2 \\
\mathcal L & = \mathcal L_\text{TopK} + w_\text{aux} \mathcal L_\text{AuxK}
\end{align}
$$
with an [AuxK term](./auxk-loss-term-sparse-autoencoders.md) to help combat [dead neurons](./dead-neurons-in-sparse-autoencoders.md).

They train a logistic regression head directly on the feature activations with $L_1$ regularisation:
$$
\min_{\boldsymbol \beta} \mathcal L(\boldsymbol{\beta}; \lambda)
= \frac{1}{N} \| \textbf y - Z_\text{SAE} \boldsymbol \beta \|_2^2
+ \lambda \| \boldsymbol \beta \|_1
$$
where $Z_\text{SAE} \in \R^{N \times M}$ is generated by concatenating the feature activations of $N$ samples. To generate exactly $H$ hypotheses they binary search values of $\lambda$ until they get a $\boldsymbol \beta$ with exactly $H$ non-zero coefficients.

To generate a feature explanation for a feature, they
1) Sample high-activation inputs (from the top decile) and low-activation inputs (from the bottom decile of inputs with non-zero activation).
2) Generate multiple explanations by prompting an LLM (GPT-4o) to identify a concept present in the high-activation inputs and missing in the low-activation inputs
3) For each explanation, they ask an LLM (GPT-4o-mini) to annotate whether 100 high-activating and 100 low-activating inputs contain the concept or not
4) They choose the explanation with the best F1 score

Low but non-zero examples are more difficult to distinguish from high-activation examples than zero-activation examples which tend to be a lot more random, resulting in more precise explanations. Connection to dense space creation / clustering / topic modelling.

They use 3 synthetic and 3 real-world datasets. The `Wiki-5` and `Wiki-15` synthetic datasets have ~12K examples. The synthetic `Bills` dataset has ~20K examples. The `Headlines` dataset has ~14K examples. They sample 200K training examples for the `Yelp` dataset. The `Congress` dataset uses 114K speech chunks for training.
